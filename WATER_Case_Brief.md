# Water Under Pressure — Europe's Water Stress as a Business Opportunity

*ATELIA × ESCP — Open Case Brief*

## Why Water, Why Now

Water has often been called "the new oil." The comparison is imperfect but useful: water is becoming a strategic input whose availability shapes economic competitiveness, industrial location, agricultural output, energy security, and political stability — not because the planet lacks water, but because usable freshwater is unevenly distributed across regions, seasons, and populations, and climate change is making that distribution more volatile.

**2026 changed the story.** By mid-August, the European Drought Observatory reported drought alert conditions across France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, southern Germany, northern Italy, and most of the Danube basin — not the usual suspects (Spain, southern Italy, Greece). Four major rivers hit record-low levels: the Loire, Po, Rhine, and Danube. Water stress has stopped being a "southern Europe" problem and become a European economic one.

**The numbers behind it:**
- ~30% of EU territory and a third of the population experience water scarcity during part of the year (EEA)
- Europe abstracts ~193 billion m³ of freshwater per year — dominated by electricity cooling, agriculture, public supply, and manufacturing
- Groundwater abstraction for agriculture rose **52%** between 2000 and 2023
- The EU's 2026 maize harvest was forecast at 50.1M tonnes — near a 20-year low, ~20% below average
- Nearly **one-third** of water abstracted for public supply is lost before reaching users (leakage above 40% in some countries)
- Only ~37% of European surface-water bodies have good ecological status; only 29% have good chemical status
- The EU's water industry already generates ~€107bn and supports ~1.7M jobs; the EIB is committing ~€15bn (2025–2027) to water resilience, aiming to mobilize ~€40bn total

## From Engineering Problem to Boardroom Issue

In July 2026, Veolia's CEO linked the company's growth directly to water security, heatwaves, and the rising water needs of AI-related industries. Veolia is developing a "Cost of No Water" methodology — because, as its CEO put it, *"the cost of not having water remains largely invisible."* In some strategic industries, a single hour of water-related production downtime can cost up to €1 million.

This reframes the market. Water itself is cheap — "save 10% on the water bill" is not a compelling pitch. What's compelling is preventing a shutdown, unlocking a new production line, staying compliant, or making a site viable in a water-constrained region. The real value of water sits closer to:

**operational continuity + avoided treatment/energy costs + regulatory compliance + future capacity + scarcity risk**

## Where the Opportunity Actually Sits

Europe already has strong water engineering (Veolia, SUEZ, Xylem, Grundfos, Schneider Electric) and strong public data (drought conditions, river basins, scarcity indicators, satellite/climate data, industrial site maps, economic data). **The gap is that these datasets are rarely integrated with operational or financial decisions.** A drought map shows hazard. A facility map shows exposure. Neither, on its own, tells anyone what to actually do.

A few directions already visible in the market:
- **Industrial water-risk platforms** — rank facilities by scarcity exposure and production sensitivity
- **Restriction-intelligence systems** — translate fragmented local drought rules into site-specific operating requirements
- **Utility investment tools** — prioritize which pipe to fix first, based on failure risk and economic impact
- **Reuse-matching platforms** — connect wastewater producers with nearby industrial demand
- **Water-finance tools** — convert engineering savings into payback periods and investable business cases

None of these replace water infrastructure. They make it more intelligible, more investable, and more responsive.

Unlike the other cases, there is no single company or decision handed to you here. **You choose the angle.** Pick a real water-related problem — for a utility, a farmer, an industrial site, a city, an insurer, a hotel chain, anyone — and build a tool that helps someone make a better decision about it, using real public data.

You are free to:
- Invent the company or organization your tool is built for
- Pick any European country, region, or sector
- Combine as many (or as few) of the public data sources as you want — see `data/PUBLIC_SOURCES.md`
- Build something narrow and deep, or something broad and exploratory

**Done when:** you have a working tool that uses at least one real public data source (even just a downloaded snapshot of it, if a live API is too heavy to integrate), addresses a specific, named user and problem, and that you can explain in plain business language — not just "it shows drought data," but *who* it helps decide *what*.
