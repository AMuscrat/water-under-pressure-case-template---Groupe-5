# Water Under Pressure: Finding Opportunity in Europe's Changing Water Economy

*ATELIA × ESCP — Open Case Brief*

On August 12, 2026, the European Commission’s Joint Research Centre (JRC) reported that prolonged rainfall deficits and repeated heatwaves had pushed four of Europe’s major rivers: the Loire, Po, Rhine, and Danube to record-low levels. Along the Rhine in Cologne, normally submerged riverbanks had become exposed. Across the continent, the implications were beginning to spread beyond the environment. Low river levels were affecting navigation, agriculture, electricity generation, water supply, tourism, and industrial activity. By late July, approximately half of the territory of the European Union and the United Kingdom was experiencing some level of drought, with France, Germany, Hungary, Romania, and the United Kingdom among the areas showing particularly severe conditions.

For many European businesses, the summer of 2026 challenged a long-standing assumption. Water had traditionally been treated as a relatively inexpensive and reliable input. A factory manager worried about energy prices, labour availability, logistics, regulation, and raw materials; water generally appeared only as another utility cost. A municipality focused on delivering clean drinking water and wastewater services. Farmers planned around seasonal rainfall. Investors rarely considered local hydrology when assessing industrial assets. Yet the drought demonstrated how quickly those assumptions could change. The same river that supplied a manufacturer could also support agriculture, electricity generation, ecosystems, tourism, municipal demand, and freight transport. When water became scarce, those users did not face separate problems. They competed for the same resource.

The drought also revealed the unusual economics of water. Unlike oil, copper, or many agricultural commodities, large volumes of water could not simply be imported from another continent when local supply tightened. Moving it is expensive. Its value depended heavily on geography, timing, and quality. One cubic metre of water flowing through a river during a wet winter can have little strategic importance. The same cubic metre during a severe summer drought might determine whether a crop survived, a factory maintained production, or a municipality imposes restrictions.

At the same time, Europe possessed one of the most developed water-service systems in the world. Across EurEau member countries, more than 550 million customers depend on drinking-water and wastewater operators to provide safe, reliable, and affordable services around the clock. Approximately 97 per cent of the population is connected to a public drinking-water network, while around 90 per cent is connected to wastewater collection and 89 per cent to wastewater treatment. The European drinking-water network stretchd roughly 4.4 million kilometres.

This apparent abundance of infrastructure conceals a more difficult question. Europe does not simply need more water. It needs to decide how to manage a resource that is becoming more variable, more contested, and more expensive to secure.

For entrepreneurs, utilities, industrial companies, policymakers, and investors, the challenge is therefore not necessarily to invent another water technology. Europe already has sophisticated treatment systems, pumps, membranes, meters, sensors, engineering firms, and global water companies. The harder problem is increasingly one of decision-making: where to invest, which water losses to address first, which users should reuse wastewater, which industrial facilities face the greatest risk, and where digital tools can create enough economic value to justify action.

## The Water Industry

Water services appear simple to the final customer. A household opens a tap, uses water, and discharges wastewater into a drain. Behind that interaction, however, stands a capital-intensive physical system involving abstraction, treatment, storage, pumping, distribution, sewer networks, wastewater treatment, laboratories, maintenance operations, regulation, and long-lived infrastructure.

The European system is also unusually heterogeneous. Water services differ according to geography, population density, access to groundwater or surface water, required treatment levels, topography, labour costs, taxes, and institutional structures. Consequently, comparing two countries based only on tariff levels or investment per capita can be misleading. EurEau cautions that national averages often conceal large differences between individual utilities and regions.

Ownership and management differ just as widely. In some jurisdictions, municipal departments both own and operate infrastructure. Elsewhere, local authorities own the assets but delegate operations to publicly owned utilities or private companies. Some systems involve public-private joint ventures, while fully private ownership remains considerably less common. The result is not a single European water market but a patchwork of national and local systems shaped by historical institutions, regulation, and municipal authority.

That structure creates a commercial complication that does not exist in many conventional industries. The party suffering from a problem may not control the budget required to solve it.

A utility engineer, for example, may identify a deteriorating section of drinking-water pipe, but a municipality or regulator may determine investment priorities. A wastewater operator may be capable of producing reclaimed water, while a nearby industrial facility controls demand for it. A farmer may benefit from more efficient irrigation but operate within water-allocation rules established by a basin authority. An industrial plant manager may recognize the operational value of water reuse, but the company’s chief financial officer may evaluate the project according to a conventional payback calculation based on relatively low water tariffs.

The relevant question is therefore not only who has a water problem.

It is who has the authority, economic incentive, and financial capacity to solve it.

## Water Becomes an Economic Constraint

For decades, European water policy was primarily associated with public health, environmental protection, and infrastructure. By the middle of the 2020s, its language was changing.

Water Europe described water as a strategic priority for European resilience, competitiveness, and security. Its concept of a “Water-Smart Economy” called for the value of water to be incorporated into product design, production systems, business strategies, investment decisions, and value chains rather than treated solely as an environmental externality.

The change reflects the number of economic systems dependent on reliable water.

Agriculture requires water for crops and livestock, particularly during the same hot and dry periods when natural supply declines. Electricity production depends on water for hydropower and cooling. Manufacturers use it for washing, cooling, steam, processing, formulation, rinsing, and contamination control. Tourism can produce sharp seasonal increases in municipal demand. Food processing, chemicals, pharmaceuticals, semiconductors, data centres, and other industries may require both reliable quantities and precise water-quality specifications.

Consequently, the strategic value of water is not necessarily reflected in the amount paid per cubic metre.

Consider a factory that purchases water cheaply from a municipal utility. A proposed efficiency project that reduces consumption by 10 per cent may generate only modest savings on the water bill. Judged on that basis, the investment may appear unattractive. If the same project reduces the probability of a production shutdown during drought restrictions, secures capacity for an additional production line, reduces wastewater-treatment requirements, or helps obtain regulatory approval for expansion, the economics change significantly.

The distinction matters because it shifts the question from the price of water to the economic consequence of losing access to it.

The European Parliament similarly links water scarcity to agriculture, food security, energy production, and competitiveness. Its 2025 assessment reports that up to 34 per cent of the EU population and 40 per cent of EU territory faced seasonal water scarcity in 2022. Around 20 per cent of European territory and 30 per cent of the population experience water stress in an average year. Southern Europe remains especially exposed, while chronic stress is also identified in countries including Cyprus, Malta, Poland, and Czechia.

The summer of 2026 broadens the geography further. The JRC reports severe rainfall deficits across central Europe, the Alps, Italy, the United Kingdom, the Balkans, and eastern Europe. Low river flows affect central and eastern Europe, northern Italy, Germany, France, and parts of Scandinavia. In Hungary and Romania, critically low Danube levels disrupt navigation, water supply, and agricultural activity; low river levels also affect hydropower and nuclear generation.

The old mental model : “water scarcity as primarily a southern-European environmental problem” is becoming increasingly difficult to defend.

## Competing for the Same Resource

Water scarcity creates a problem that is simultaneously physical, economic, and political because different sectors often require additional water at the same moment.

A farmer may need irrigation precisely when temperatures are highest. Households may use more water during the same period. Hotels and tourism destinations can experience peak demand. A power station may require additional cooling. Industrial facilities may be unable to reduce production without significant financial losses. River systems still require enough water to support ecosystems and navigation.

The question therefore shifts from “How much water is available?” to “Who receives water, when, in what quantity, at what quality, and with what consequences?”

The European Parliament estimates that cooling for electricity generation accounts for approximately 34 per cent of EU freshwater abstraction, agriculture 29 per cent, public water supply 21 per cent, and manufacturing 15 per cent. These percentages also demonstrate why water cannot easily be analysed as a single-industry problem.

Each user values water differently. A household expects safe and affordable supply. A municipality prioritizes continuity and public health. A farmer may compare the cost of irrigation with the value of the expected crop. A chemical plant may measure water against production output worth millions of euros. A regulator must consider ecological flows and water quality. A utility must balance affordability with infrastructure investment.

The allocation problem is therefore not simply technical. It is a problem of competing economic objectives.

## Infrastructure Under Strain

While drought and heat are making the availability of water more uncertain, another problem is building much more slowly beneath Europe’s cities and towns. The continent’s drinking-water network stretches approximately 4.4 million kilometres across EurEau member countries, including around 3.9 million kilometres in the EU27. Much of this infrastructure was installed decades ago and requires continuous maintenance, rehabilitation, and replacement. Because water networks are largely buried underground, deterioration can remain invisible until a pipe fails, pressure drops, or treated water is lost before reaching customers. Geography also complicates the problem: network length per inhabitant varies considerably across countries depending on settlement patterns, population density, topography, and the extent of rural connections.

For European water operators, infrastructure renewal has therefore become one of the sector’s most important strategic concerns. EurEau members rank it as their leading challenge, ahead of a broader set of pressures that includes climate adaptation, financing, pollution, and regulatory compliance. The difficulty is not simply that pipes are ageing. Utilities are being asked to renew infrastructure at the same time that treatment requirements are becoming more demanding. Emerging contaminants such as PFAS and other micropollutants increasingly require sophisticated treatment, while more established pollutants such as nitrates and pesticides continue to affect drinking-water resources. As a result, available capital must be divided between maintaining old assets, meeting stricter standards, adapting to climate change, and investing in new technologies.

The financial pressure is substantial. Water-service providers across the countries covered by EurEau invest approximately €52.5 billion per year in infrastructure, of which around €36.6 billion is invested within the EU27. Although overall investment has increased compared with the previous reporting period, the rise in the EU27 is only around 5 per cent and remains below inflation. EurEau interprets this as evidence of growing budget constraints and an investment backlog in a number of countries. On average, European providers invest roughly €101 per inhabitant per year, although national differences are considerable. These figures illustrate a structural problem: the scale of the network is enormous, but the financial resources available to renew it are limited.

For a utility manager, the problem is therefore less straightforward than simply replacing old pipes. A network may contain thousands of kilometres of assets of different ages, materials, diameters, and operating conditions. Some pipes may have failed repeatedly but serve relatively few customers; others may show few warning signs but supply hospitals, industrial zones, or districts exposed to severe drought. A fifty-year-old pipe losing water in a low-density, water-abundant region may be less urgent than a younger pipe whose failure could interrupt supply to a hospital or a factory in a water-stressed basin. Faced with a fixed capital budget, managers must decide not only which assets are oldest, but where failure would create the greatest operational, economic, or social consequences.

Digitalisation is beginning to improve the information available for those decisions. Meter coverage is already high in many European countries, but the adoption of automatic remote reading remains uneven, with only four countries in EurEau’s dataset reporting smart-meter coverage above 50 per cent. Utilities are also measuring real losses and non-revenue water more systematically, using indicators that allow them to compare leakage and network performance more consistently. Yet greater visibility does not remove the investment constraint. A utility may know which parts of its network are losing water and still be unable to repair all of them. The more valuable question therefore moves beyond detection toward prioritisation: given limited capital, which intervention should happen first, and why?

This represents an important shift in the economics of digital water. Sensors, meters, hydraulic models, and analytics can help operators observe the network more precisely, but observation alone does not determine where scarce investment should be allocated. The opportunity increasingly lies in combining technical information, such as pipe age, pressure, failure history, leakage, and material with contextual information such as water scarcity, critical customers, repair cost, and the consequences of service interruption. In that sense, the problem facing utilities is not simply a lack of data. It is the difficulty of turning data into an investment decision.

## Water Quality: Available Does Not Mean Usable

Scarcity is only one dimension of Europe’s water problem. Quality creates a second constraint. In 2021, only 37 per cent of European surface-water bodies achieved good or high ecological status, while just 29 per cent achieved good chemical status. The European Parliament identifies nutrients, pesticides, industrial substances, and other contaminants as persistent pressures on European water resources, while water operators increasingly point to PFAS and other micropollutants as major concerns alongside more established pollutants such as nitrates and pesticides.

For industry, this matters because not all water is interchangeable. A semiconductor plant, brewery, pharmaceutical facility, farm, cooling system, and municipal park each require different quality characteristics. Water that is unsuitable for drinking may still be useful for irrigation or cooling, while water that appears plentiful may be unusable for a particular industrial process because of salinity, conductivity, temperature, microorganisms, or trace contaminants. More complex water quality also means more treatment, greater energy consumption, additional equipment, and higher operating costs.

The result is an important economic distinction: physical availability does not necessarily mean usable availability. A cubic metre of water creates value only if it is available in the right place, at the right time, and at a quality suitable for the intended use—or can be treated economically to reach that standard. This distinction is becoming increasingly important as Europe looks to water reuse as a way of reducing pressure on freshwater resources.

## From Wastewater to Resource

The traditional urban water system is largely linear: water is abstracted, treated, distributed, consumed, collected, treated again as wastewater, and then discharged back into the environment. Under increasing scarcity, however, this model is becoming less attractive. A wastewater-treatment plant may release large volumes of treated effluent into a river while an industrial facility only a few kilometres away continues to purchase freshwater; similarly, municipalities may use potable water for landscaping while reclaimed water remains available nearby. In that context, wastewater increasingly looks less like an end product and more like a potential secondary resource.

Europe has already begun to develop reuse systems, but adoption remains uneven. EurEau’s 2026 data show Spain producing by far the largest reported volume of treated wastewater for reuse, while Cyprus reports a reuse rate close to 100 per cent. Smaller schemes are also present across Northern and Central Europe, and reclaimed water is used for agricultural irrigation, industrial applications, and urban or leisure purposes.

Yet the existence of treatment technology does not make every reuse project viable. A workable project requires sufficient volume, compatible timing of supply and demand, acceptable water quality, manageable transport distance, reasonable pumping and treatment costs, and a regulatory framework that permits the intended use. A wastewater plant producing 10 million cubic metres of treated effluent annually may appear to be a natural supplier for a factory consuming 3 million cubic metres five kilometres away, but additional treatment, storage requirements, pipeline construction, or low freshwater prices can quickly weaken the economics. The challenge is therefore not simply whether reuse is technically possible, but whether a specific connection creates enough value to justify the investment.

This opens a distinct business opportunity before any infrastructure is built: identifying which potential reuse projects are worth investigating first.

## The Economics of the Utility

Utilities face a different but equally important tension. Europe has made progress in reducing drinking-water use: EurEau reports that billed consumption and residential per-capita consumption have both fallen by about 9 per cent compared with its previous report, while drinking-water production has declined by around 5 per cent. From a resilience perspective, this is positive. Financially, however, lower consumption can reduce revenue even though many operating costs—networks, treatment plants, staff, energy systems, and maintenance—remain largely fixed.

Water services are financed through a combination of tariffs, taxes, and transfers, with tariffs usually providing the largest and most stable source of income. Across the countries covered by EurEau, annual billing reaches approximately €109.2 billion, an increase of only 8 per cent from the previous reporting period despite substantially higher inflation. At the same time, households in most countries spend roughly 1 per cent of their income on water services, preserving affordability but limiting the ability of utilities to raise prices aggressively to fund investment.

The result is a structural business-model tension. Utilities are expected to encourage lower consumption while financing a capital-intensive system through revenues partly linked to water use. They must renew ageing assets, comply with stricter treatment standards, adapt to climate risks, and maintain affordability at the same time. For a service that society expects to function continuously and almost invisibly, the economics are becoming increasingly difficult to balance.

## Digital Water

As financial, regulatory, and climate pressures accumulate, digitalisation offers water operators and users a different route to improvement. The sector has traditionally been dominated by physical infrastructure such as pumps, pipes, reservoirs, treatment plants, and laboratories, but an increasingly important digital layer is being built around those assets. Remote meters can reveal consumption patterns, pressure sensors can identify abnormal network behaviour, satellite imagery can track drought and vegetation stress, and geospatial systems can connect water resources with industrial sites, farms, utilities, and population centres. Artificial intelligence and digital twins add another dimension by helping operators detect anomalies, model scenarios, and anticipate system behaviour.

European policy increasingly encourages this transition. The European Parliament highlights real time monitoring, artificial intelligence, smart irrigation, water efficiency technologies, and advanced treatment as important tools for improving resilience, while Water Europe places digitalisation at the centre of its implementation agenda through initiatives intended to improve the use of water data, investment, skills, and industrial coordination. The direction is clear: Europe is not only modernising physical infrastructure, but also seeking to make water systems more observable and more responsive.

Yet better data does not automatically produce better decisions. A drought platform may show that a basin is under stress, a geographic database may identify nearby industrial facilities, a utility may know the age and failure history of its pipes, and a wastewater operator may know how much treated effluent it discharges each day. Each dataset is useful, but each describes only part of the problem. The commercial opportunity increasingly lies in combining them so that a manager can move from knowing what is happening to deciding what action to take.

## The Stakeholder Problem

Technology alone cannot explain why attractive water projects often fail to move forward. The European water system is shaped by fragmented incentives, with the organisation experiencing a problem often differing from the organisation controlling the budget or receiving the wider benefit. A utility may reduce leakage, but a municipality may determine capital spending. An industrial company may reduce freshwater abstraction, while part of the benefit accrues to other users in the same basin. A wastewater operator may have reclaimed water available but lack the authority or financing to connect it to a nearby industrial customer.

This fragmentation complicates the market for new solutions. A plant engineer may value a drought risk tool but lack purchasing authority; a utility may want a leakage platform but require municipal procurement approval; a reuse project may depend on permits, financing, long term contracts, and agreement between several independent organisations. In practice, the user, buyer, asset owner, regulator, and economic beneficiary may all be different stakeholders. For entrepreneurs, understanding who experiences the problem is therefore only the first step. They must also identify who can authorize, pay for, and capture enough value from the solution.

## Europe’s Water-Smart Economy

By 2026, European policy makers increasingly frame water as a question of competitiveness and resilience rather than environmental protection alone. Water Europe’s concept of a Water Smart Economy calls for water considerations to be integrated into investment, industrial strategy, innovation, and value chains, while the European Parliament similarly emphasizes efficiency, reuse, infrastructure modernisation, monitoring, and cross border cooperation. The policy message is increasingly consistent: water has become part of Europe’s economic and industrial strategy.

Investment pressures reinforce that shift. Almost all EurEau members responding to its 2026 survey expect investment needs to rise over the following five years, with most anticipating increases of more than 10 per cent. Infrastructure renewal, regulatory compliance, climate adaptation, and circular economy investment are all expected to absorb additional capital.

The strategic opportunity is therefore not difficult to see, but it remains difficult to define precisely. More capital is likely to enter the sector, more regulation will require monitoring and compliance, climate volatility will increase the value of resilience, and digitalisation will make more data available. The unresolved question is where a new solution can turn those changes into enough operational or economic value to matter.

## Looking Ahead

By the second half of 2026, Europe’s water industry is entering a period in which scarcity, ageing infrastructure, pollution, investment constraints, reuse, and digitalisation are becoming increasingly interconnected. The sector does not lack engineering capability, but it still struggles to direct limited capital and fragmented information toward the most valuable interventions.

For students examining the industry, the central challenge is therefore not to solve Europe’s water problem as a whole, but to identify one specific decision, one stakeholder, and one problem where better information or a simple digital tool can materially improve the outcome.

## The Student Challenge: From Problem to Prototype

Your task is not to solve Europe’s water problem as a whole. It is to identify one specific problem within the water system and determine whether a simple digital solution can help someone make a better decision. Start with the stakeholder, not the technology. Ask yourself who experiences the problem, what decision that person or organisation needs to make, what information is missing, what happens if the decision is poor, and who has the incentive and budget to pay for a better solution.

Once you can define that problem clearly, technology becomes relevant. AI assisted development, often described as vibe coding, allows you to build simple applications by describing what you want the software to do in natural language and iterating with an AI coding system. You do not need to recreate professional water engineering software. The objective is to test whether a narrowly defined problem can be translated into a usable prototype that improves a real decision.

For example, if you focus on industrial drought risk, you might build a tool in which a manager enters the location of a factory and receives a simple risk assessment based on public drought information and basic site characteristics. The purpose is not to predict Europe’s hydrology in detail, but to help answer a practical question: which facilities deserve management attention first?

If you focus on water reuse, you might map wastewater treatment plants and nearby industrial sites, compare distance and available volumes, and identify which potential connections are worth investigating. If you focus on utility infrastructure, you might combine pipe age, failure history, location, and the presence of critical users to help a utility decide which assets should receive replacement capital first.

The strongest project is not necessarily the one with the most features, the most artificial intelligence, or the largest number of datasets. It is the one built around the clearest problem and the most specific decision. By the end of the exercise, you should be able to describe your target problem in a single sentence. If you cannot, the problem is probably still too broad.
